## THE 1-MINUTE SCRIPT
*(138 words, ~58 seconds)*

There are three management books everyone references separately, but they're actually one system.

Donella Meadows wrote "Thinking in Systems" — the theory. She says: everything is interconnected. Feedback loops steer systems. The most powerful interventions don't change the numbers — they change who sees what.

Andy Grove wrote "High Output Management" — the practice. He says: your output is your team's output. Find the bottleneck. Manage through leverage, not effort.

John Doerr wrote "Measure What Matters" — the instrument. He took Grove's OKR system from Intel to Google. The mechanism isn't the goals — it's transparency. When everyone can see everyone else's goals, the system self-corrects.

Read separately, they're three books. Read together: Meadows explains WHY they work, Grove tells you HOW to manage, Doerr gives you the TOOL.

Start with the lens. Then the manual. Then the instrument.

## RECIPE CARD

### Ingredients (the concepts)

1. **Systems thinking (Meadows):** A system is more than its parts — it's the connections, feedback loops, and delays between them. You can't understand a traffic jam by studying individual cars. The most powerful leverage points change information flows and rules, not parameters.

2. **Managerial leverage (Grove):** A manager's output is the output of their team plus the teams they influence. Some activities have high leverage (a decision that 200 people execute) and some are low (an email one person reads). Find the limiting step — the bottleneck that sets the pace of everything downstream.

3. **OKRs as designed feedback (Doerr):** Objectives (what you want, ambitious, qualitative) + Key Results (how you'll measure it, specific, time-bound). The power isn't the goal itself — it's transparency. When everyone can see everyone else's goals, the organization self-aligns without central control.

### How they combine

The non-obvious claim: Meadows's systems theory explains why Grove's and Doerr's practices work.

- Grove says "find the limiting step." Meadows says "systems have bottlenecks governed by structure, not components." Same insight, different vocabulary.
- Doerr says "make OKRs transparent." Meadows says "the most powerful interventions change information flows." OKR transparency IS an intervention in the information structure of an organization — it changes who sees what, and that changes how the system behaves.
- Grove says "your output is the team's output." Meadows says "you can't optimize a system by optimizing each part independently." Both reject individual-hero management in favor of managing the system.

The progression: Meadows gives you the lens (why). Grove gives you the manual (how). Doerr gives you the instrument (what to use). Reading them in that order makes each one more meaningful than the last.

### What someone can do with it

1. **Before setting goals for your team, map the system.** Draw the feedback loops: where does information come from, how fast does it arrive, who sees it, what delays exist. If your lagging indicators (quarterly results) arrive months after the behavior that produced them, you're managing a shower that's always too hot or too cold. OKRs work because they shorten that loop — you see progress weekly, not quarterly.

2. **When you feel busy but unproductive, check leverage not effort.** List everything you did today. For each item ask: how many people does this affect, and for how long? A one-on-one where you teach a skill that compounds for a year is high leverage. Answering an email that only one person reads is low. Optimize the mix, not the hours.

### What I got wrong / What I'm uncertain about

- The claim that Meadows "explains why" Grove and Doerr work is my synthesis, not something any of the authors claim. It's plausible but it's an interpretation. A systems scientist might say I'm stretching the connection — Grove and Doerr are management practices, not formal system dynamics models.
- I compressed Meadows's "12 leverage points" into "change information flows" — that's one of the mid-to-high leverage points but not the highest (the highest is changing the paradigm). Simplified for the talk; the recipe card should note this.
- I didn't mention CASL or any of the practical constraints — but this is a book synthesis, not an operational talk, so that's fine.
- The "start with the lens" reading order is my recommendation, not a canonical one. Most people read Doerr first because it's the most popular. I think Meadows → Grove → Doerr is more illuminating but I can't prove it's better.
