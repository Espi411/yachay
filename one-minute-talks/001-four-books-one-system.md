## THE 1-MINUTE SCRIPT
*(~145 words, ~60 seconds)*

There are four management books everyone reads separately, but they're actually one system.

Donella Meadows wrote "Thinking in Systems" — the lens. Everything is interconnected. Feedback loops steer systems. The most powerful interventions don't change the numbers — they change who sees what.

Eliyahu Goldratt wrote "The Goal" — the method. Every system has one bottleneck, and that bottleneck sets the throughput of everything. Find it, exploit it, subordinate everything else to it.

Andy Grove wrote "High Output Management" — the manual. Your output is your team's output. Manage through leverage, not effort.

John Doerr wrote "Measure What Matters" — the tool. He took Grove's OKRs from Intel to Google. The mechanism isn't the goals — it's transparency. When everyone can see everyone else's goals, the system self-corrects.

Lens, method, manual, tool. That's one system, not four.

## RECIPE CARD

### Ingredients (the concepts)

1. **Systems thinking (Meadows):** A system is more than its parts — it's the connections, feedback loops, and delays between them. You can't understand a traffic jam by studying individual cars. The most powerful leverage points change information flows and rules, not parameters.

2. **Theory of Constraints (Goldratt):** Every system has exactly one constraint at any time, and that constraint determines the throughput of the entire system. The Five Focusing Steps: identify the constraint, exploit it (squeeze maximum throughput from it as-is), subordinate everything else to it (don't let fast steps build inventory), elevate it (invest to increase its capacity), repeat (when you fix one bottleneck, another appears). Local optimization — making each step efficient independently — is the enemy of system optimization.

3. **Managerial leverage (Grove):** A manager's output is the output of their team plus the teams they influence. Some activities have high leverage (a decision that 200 people execute) and some are low (an email one person reads). Find the limiting step — the bottleneck that sets the pace of everything downstream.

4. **OKRs as designed feedback (Doerr):** Objectives (what you want, ambitious, qualitative) + Key Results (how you'll measure it, specific, time-bound). The power isn't the goal itself — it's transparency. When everyone can see everyone else's goals, the organization self-aligns without central control.

### How they combine

The non-obvious claim: these four books form a complete stack — theory, method, practice, instrument — and each layer makes the others more powerful.

- Meadows gives you the lens: systems have bottlenecks governed by structure, not components. Goldratt gives you the method for actually finding and managing that bottleneck. Same insight, but Goldratt makes it operational with the Five Focusing Steps.
- Grove names the "limiting step" — Goldratt's constraint, recognized in a knowledge-work context. But Grove goes further: he tells you how to manage people around it (leverage, task-relevant maturity, one-on-ones, reviews).
- Doerr's OKRs are the tool that makes the constraint visible to the whole organization. When OKRs are transparent, everyone can see where the bottleneck is and self-organize around it. This is Meadows's "intervene in the information structure" made concrete.
- Goldratt's fifth step — "when you fix one bottleneck, another appears" — is Meadows's system dynamics in action. You never solve it once; you manage the sequence. OKRs help you see the new constraint quickly because the feedback loop is short.

The progression: Meadows (why) → Goldratt (method) → Grove (how to manage people) → Doerr (the tool). Read in that order, each one makes the next more meaningful.

### What someone can do with it

1. **Before setting goals for your team, find the constraint.** Don't set OKRs until you know what the bottleneck is — otherwise you'll optimize non-constraints and build inventory (in a knowledge-work context: work-in-progress that piles up at the slow step). Walk through Goldratt's five steps: identify, exploit, subordinate, elevate, repeat. THEN set OKRs that focus on breaking the constraint, not on making everyone "productive."

2. **When you feel busy but unproductive, check the constraint, not your effort.** If you're working on a non-constraint, you're building inventory — busy work that doesn't increase throughput. Ask: what is the one thing in this system that, if I improved it, would increase overall output? That's the constraint. Everything else is secondary until that's addressed.

### What I got wrong / What I'm uncertain about

- The claim that these four form "one system" is my synthesis, not something any of the authors claim. The Grove→Doerr lineage is documented; the Meadows and Goldratt connections are my interpretation. A specialist might argue Goldratt's TOC is more tightly formalized than I'm giving it credit for, or that Meadows's leverage points framework doesn't map cleanly onto Goldratt's focusing steps.
- I compressed Goldratt's Five Focusing Steps into a few seconds of talk. The "subordinate" step is the most counterintuitive and the most important — and it got the least airtime. If someone pushed back, I'd say: the recipe card carries the detail; the talk carries the arc.
- The "one bottleneck at a time" claim is Goldratt's, but in practice systems can have multiple interacting constraints. Goldratt would say pick the dominant one and work it; critics say reality is messier. I sided with Goldratt for the talk.
- I didn't mention Deming, who is upstream of all four. That's a defensible cut for a 1-minute talk but a real omission in the recipe card — see the full synthesis for the Deming connection.
